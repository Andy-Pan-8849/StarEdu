# AI in industry and its effect on jobs: an evidence list with sources

[中文](ai-industry-evidence.chn.md)

Compiled on 2026-10-31. Purpose: to understand how AI is really being deployed in companies and what effect it actually has on employment.
Every item is marked with its level of verification: **original source checked** / **secondary reports only** / **not verified**.

## 1. Employment: layoff data

**Source**: the monthly report of Challenger, Gray & Christmas (layoffs publicly announced by US employers, counting the reasons the employers themselves give).

| Report month | Layoffs with AI listed as the reason (that month) | Year to date | Share of all layoffs | Verification |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| July 2026 | 10,970 (33% of the month) | 112,713 | about 24% | Original page checked |
| August 2026 | 3,462 | 116,175 | about 22% | Search summary only, the report PDF was not opened |

- Layoffs attributed to AI cumulatively since 2023: 184,538 (July report).
- All layoffs in July were 33,429, and 477,033 year to date; in August 529,914 year to date, about 41% lower than the same period last year (the August figures are from a summary and were not checked).
- From August AI is no longer the first reason, and "restructuring" is back in front.
- The report itself says that AI attribution is ambiguous, and some layoffs are recorded as "technology update (possibly related to AI)". **This is the reason the companies claim, not independent proof.**
- Note: a blog online says "120,136 people from January to September"; I could not find the original September report, so this is **not verified and must not be cited**.

## 2. Employment: entry-level jobs

**Stanford Digital Economy Lab, "Canaries in the Coal Mine?"** (Brynjolfsson, Chandar, Chen)

- Uses administrative data from the largest payroll software provider in the US.
- Employees aged 22 to 25 in the **occupations most exposed to AI automation** saw employment fall relative to others: 13% in the August 2025 version and 16% in the November 2025 update. **The figure changes with the version, so state the version when citing it.**
- More experienced employees in the same occupations, and occupations with low AI exposure, saw stable or growing employment.
- The decline is concentrated in occupations where "AI replaces people" and not in those where "AI assists people".
- Verification: the abstract on the paper's page, from search results; **no independent replication or refutation was found**, so the conclusion rests mainly on the authors' own paper.

Link: [paper page](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publications/canaries-in-the-coal-mine)

Note: figures in secondary blogs such as "entry-level hiring down 73.4%" and "21% of companies have frozen entry-level hiring" come from a single region or a single survey and are **not verified**.

## 3. Productivity: a controlled study in customer service

**NBER Working Paper 31161, "Generative AI at Work"** (Brynjolfsson, Li, Raymond); later published in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, volume 140, issue 2.

- Subjects: about 5,000 customer-service agents using an AI assistant.
- Result: the number of issues resolved per hour rose by about 14% on average (an early version says 15%).
- Novice and low-skill employees improved by about 34%, with almost no effect on experienced employees.
- Better customer sentiment and lower employee turnover were also observed.
- Verification: the abstract of the paper from search results, **the full text was not read**.

Links: [NBER page](https://nber.org/papers/w31161), [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4426942)

## 4. A counterexample: Klarna

- The company had made a big push to replace customer service with AI. The CEO later told Bloomberg that AI customer service was cheaper but of lower quality, and the company hired human agents again (reported as a remote, part-time kind of model).
- AI chatbots still handle about two thirds of inquiries, so it is a **hybrid model and not a full withdrawal**.
- The figures the company claims for its AI gains (such as "2.3 million conversations a month") are reported by the company itself and **have not been independently audited**.
- Verification: secondary reports from several media only, **the Bloomberg original was not read**; the reports disagree on details and timing.

Links: [Tech.co](https://tech.co/news/klarna-reverses-ai-overhaul), [Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/business-news/klarna-ceo-reverses-course-by-hiring-more-humans-not-ai/491396), [Maginative](https://www.maginative.com/article/klarna-dials-back-its-ai-customer-service-strategy-now-its-hiring-humans-again/)

## 5. Degree of enterprise adoption (secondary, to be checked yourself)

The search results relayed a McKinsey survey: about nine in ten companies regularly use AI in at least one function; most people feel their personal productivity has risen, but **the share reporting an effect on profit is clearly lower**.
- Verification: **all from secondary blogs, and the McKinsey original was not read**. I give no specific percentages; please look directly at McKinsey's "The state of AI" page.
- Likewise, figures circulating online such as "customer-service cost down 25%, satisfaction up 20%" **could not be checked against the original** and must not be cited.

## 6. What was not verified this time

- Specific company cases in various industries (law, healthcare, finance, manufacturing and so on): the examples given in the earlier conversation came from my existing knowledge and **were not checked online this time**.
- The full text of Challenger's August and September reports, Klarna's Bloomberg original, the McKinsey survey original.

## 7. How to read these data (a checklist for yourself)

1. Is it **tasks** or **jobs** that are being replaced?
2. Is there human backup? The Klarna example shows that quality problems make a company turn back.
3. Who is the data from: researchers, a statistics body, or vendors and the companies themselves?
4. "The company says it laid people off because of AI" and "AI caused the layoffs" are two different things.
